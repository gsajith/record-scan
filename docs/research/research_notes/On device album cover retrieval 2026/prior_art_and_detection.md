# Prior art for camera-based record identification and sleeve isolation without a custom detector (as of October 2026)

Scope note. Most app-level evidence comes from App Store and Play listings, vendor blogs and competitor comparison posts. Independent accuracy tests of record-scanning apps do not exist in what I found. Numbers below are tagged with their source and date where the source gives one.

## Which apps identify records by cover photo in 2026, how they do it, and what users complain about

### Takeaway
The incumbents (Discogs, CLZ Music) still identify records by barcode, catalog number or text search, not by cover photo. Cover-photo recognition lives in a crowded field of 2025 and 2026 subscription apps whose main user complaints are wrong pressings, inflated prices and paywalls, not failure to find the album. Release-family identification from the sleeve is treated as solved. Pressing-level identification from the sleeve is not.

### Cited Findings
- The official Discogs iOS and Android apps offer a barcode scanner (camera icon next to the search bar). Discogs' own help articles warn that not all releases in the database have barcodes, so a scan may return nothing. [Discogs iOS help](https://support.discogs.com/hc/en-us/articles/360014043894-How-To-Search-In-The-Database-When-Using-The-Discogs-iOS-App); [Discogs Android help](https://support.discogs.com/hc/en-us/articles/360013820574-How-To-Search-In-The-Database-When-Using-The-Discogs-Android-App)
- I found no official Discogs cover-image recognition feature. Third-party apps such as My Vinyl+ wrap Discogs data but list barcode scanning, not cover recognition. [My Vinyl+ App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/my-vinyl-scanner-for-discogs/id1547173908)
- A community thread reports that Discogs barcode scans can return several candidate releases, and users compare listing photos against the physical record to pick one. [Naim community thread](https://community.naimaudio.com/t/discogs-tips-tricks-hacks-and-help/43421)
- A VinylAI comparison (updated 2 August 2026, written by a competitor) says "Barcode scans do not always resolve to the correct pressing" in the Discogs app and calls the app slow and technical for casual users. It says barcode-led tools struggle with older LPs and same-barcode reissues. [VinylAI best vinyl apps 2026](https://vinylai.app/guides/best-vinyl-record-apps/)
- CLZ Music identifies records by barcode (listings claim a 95% or a "guaranteed 99%" barcode success rate), artist and title search, and catalog number search. I found no cover-photo recognition. It costs US $1.99 per month or $19.99 per year. [CLZ Music App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/clz-music-cd-vinyl-database/id317997093); [APKMirror CLZ listing](https://www.apkmirror.com/apk/collectorz-com/clz-music-cd-vinyl-database)
- Record Scanner (Vinyl and CD) scans barcodes, catalog numbers via OCR, and cover images, with an inner-label scanner in development. Microsoft's Cosmos DB blog (17 September 2025) says it reached "a quarter of a million downloads". The post describes moving vector search to Azure Cosmos DB and running embedding models in the browser with transformers.js. It does not name the embedding model, cropping step, latency or accuracy. [Azure Cosmos DB blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cosmosdb/record-scanner-for-vinyl-collectors-cuts-costs-with-azure-cosmos-db-vector-search/)
- That post's cost example assumes 15M vectors: Cosmos DB at $85 per month against $1,950 per month for a 256 GB RAM Ebdsv5 VM. It does not say how many vectors Record Scanner actually stores. [Azure Cosmos DB blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cosmosdb/record-scanner-for-vinyl-collectors-cuts-costs-with-azure-cosmos-db-vector-search/)
- Record Scanner App Store reviews: one professional buyer says prices are "heavily inflated", with $1 yard-sale records shown at $5 to $7. Another user says identification "nailed all of the records in the free trial". An aggregator quotes a user saying the app does not disclose that only 30 scans are free before a fee. [Record Scanner App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/record-scanner-vinyl-cd/id1507752204?see-all=reviews&platform=ipad); [AppsHunter page](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/record-scanner-vinyl-and-cd/id1507752204)
- VinylSnap reviews report wrong pressings, wrong records entirely and incorrect values, plus trial-to-subscription surprises. A Google Play review of Vinyl ID says it charged for a full year up front with no advertised 3-day trial. [VinylSnap App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/vinylsnap-scan-value-record/id6748595105?see-all=reviews&platform=ipad); [Vinyl ID Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.britetodo.vinylsnap&hl=en_US)
- Many 2025 and 2026 listings (Vinyl Record Scanner, Vinyl Identifier, Vinyl Snap, DiscSnap, Scan & Value Record, CD Scanner, Albumzy) advertise "AI" cover recognition plus pressing, condition grade and value. Vinyl Record Scanner's own listing calls its results "AI-powered estimates based on visual analysis". Several listings show a last update of 15 May 2026. These are marketing claims with no published accuracy. [Vinyl Record Scanner listing](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/vinyl-record-scanner/6758638904?country=hk); [Vinyl Identifier listing](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/vinyl-identifier-records-scan/6757678539?country=us); [DiscSnap listing](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/discsnap-vinyl-record-scanner/6757983137?country=jp); [Albumzy](https://apps.apple.com/mx/app/albumzy/id6740087713)
- Record Scanner for Vinyl & CD (Vinyl Snap) gives new users 2 free searches. Unlimited scanning requires a subscription. [Record Scanner for Vinyl & CD listing](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/record-scanner-for-vinyl-cd/6758935180?country=vg)
- VinylAI's guide says a sleeve photo is usually enough to identify the "release family" and a shortlist of editions, but no app is 100% accurate on pressing-level ID from cover art alone. It notes that many collectible records pressed before about 1982 have no barcode. The source is a vendor. [VinylAI guide](https://vinylai.app/guides/can-an-app-identify-a-vinyl-record-from-a-photo/)
- Apple Visual Look Up (iOS 15 and later, in Photos and the camera's recent-photo view) recognizes album covers among plants, animals, landmarks, books and art. This source dates from 2021. [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/351513/ios-15-how-to-search-inside-your-photos-using-visual-look-up.html). A lower-quality explainer claims album-cover results can link into Apple Music; I could not confirm this. [fmyly explainer](https://en.fmyly.com/article/where-is-visual-look-up-on-my-iphone/)
- Older precedent (2018): Patrick Weaver's "Record Player" web app sent a photo to Google Cloud Vision and then searched Spotify. A reviewer reported it got Led Zeppelin IV on the first try and named Tom Waits but struggled with the exact album (Rain Dogs). [Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/record-player/); [Ubergizmo](https://www.ubergizmo.com/2018/05/record-player-shazam-album-covers)

### Inferences
- The gap in the market is not "can it find the album". It is speed to a result, honest uncertainty between pressings, and no paywall at scan two. A free scan-to-preview app with a fast result has a clear angle against the subscription value-estimator apps.
- Record Scanner, the most-downloaded cover-scanning app with any public technical detail, uses embeddings plus a hosted vector database. Server-side vector search over tens of millions of vectors costs about $85 per month by Microsoft's own example, which bounds the hybrid option's hosting cost.
- No app publishes that it uses a sleeve detector. Their pipelines are not public, so this says nothing about whether they use one.

### Gaps
- No independent accuracy or latency benchmark of any record-scanning app, Google Lens or Visual Look Up on album covers.
- No source on whether Shazam has any visual cover recognition. I found none.
- Google Play reviews could not be fetched (the page returned placeholder content), so Android-side complaints are thin.

## Open-source and research projects on album cover recognition and music artwork retrieval

### Takeaway
Open-source prior art is small and mostly hobby-grade: perceptual hashing (image-match) that only works on near-identical images, and a few CLIP or MobileCLIP experiments. The most relevant industrial write-up is Deezer's 2018 cover-art fingerprinting for catalog matching, which handled photos and scans of physical covers. Nobody has published a phone-photo-to-cover retrieval benchmark.

### Cited Findings
- project-33t (boehm-s) recognizes covers with the image-match library and fetches price data from the Discogs API. Its README says matching "seems to work only for near-identical images". Cropping the input to a square is still a TODO. It picked image-match because the approach "has proven successful at Deezer". [GitHub project-33t](https://github.com/boehm-s/project-33t)
- Deezer's January 2018 post describes cover-art fingerprinting for matching albums across catalogs, as a complement to error-prone title and artist matching. It expects the worst input to include "photographs or scans of CD/vinyl cover with possible truncations, empty spaces and color changes". It cites the Wong, Bern and Goldberg (2002) "image signature for any kind of image". [Deezer newsroom](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2018/01/matching-albums-through-cover-art-fingerprinting/)
- A March 2024 blog post catalogued a record collection from webcam frames using Apple's MobileCLIP as a zero-shot "vinyl record" vs "something else" classifier. It saved a frame once a record stayed in view and used cosine similarity on embeddings to skip duplicates. I saw only the search snippet; the page returned HTTP 403. [jamesg.blog](https://jamesg.blog/2024/03/16/vinyl-record-indexing)
- Crate Scanner, a student capstone, detects covers and returns Spotify samples, Discogs prices and recommendations; its image model outputs album and artist name. [Hashnode write-up](https://wesleyhouse.hashnode.dev/building-a-vinyl-image-recognition-and-recommendation-application-using-python)
- Other work is adjacent rather than retrieval: genre classification from covers with a CNN trained on scraped Discogs data [GitHub kasperjunge](https://github.com/kasperjunge/Deep-Learning-Vinyl-Records); artist similarity and genre tagging from cover art [Libeks, IEEE MM 2011](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~libeks/Libeks_ArtistImage_IEEEMM11.pdf); a 2006 JKU paper on retrieving covers from the web via Google and A9 image search [JKU](https://research.jku.at/en/publications/towards-automatic-retrieval-of-album-covers/).
- Cover-art downloaders such as SACAD and CoverLovin do metadata lookup, not image matching. [SACAD on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/sacad/1.6.1/)
- An Internet Archive collection of about one million album cover images (around 148 GB) exists for research use. [beSpacific](https://www.bespacific.com/?p=45241)
- A USPTO patent covers detecting album art in images to retrieve metadata and playlists using a classifier. [US 11263493](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11263493)

### Inferences
- Gautham's 3.13M Cover Art Archive gallery is larger than any public album-cover retrieval set I found, and no published method beats a modern global embedding plus re-ranking on this task. The project will need its own phone-photo evaluation set; nobody else has one to borrow.
- Perceptual hashes fail on phone photos because of perspective, glare and background. That matches project-33t's finding and argues for learned embeddings.

### Gaps
- No GitHub repo or paper found that reports recall numbers for phone photos of sleeves against a cover database.
- The jamesg.blog post could not be read in full.

## Sleeve isolation without a custom model on iOS and Android

### Takeaway
iOS has two first-party quad detectors that return corner points for perspective correction: the classic VNDetectRectanglesRequest (CPU edge-based, tunable aspect ratio) and the ML-based document segmentation request (Neural Engine, corners plus mask). Android has no first-party equivalent that exposes corners: ML Kit's document scanner is a fixed Google Play services UI, and ML Kit object detection returns only coarse boxes. Cross-platform corner detection on Android means OpenCV contours. No source measures any of these on glossy square sleeves.

### Cited Findings
- VNDetectRectanglesRequest (iOS 11+) finds "projected rectangular regions" such as cards, documents and signs, and returns VNRectangleObservation with normalized corner coordinates and a confidence. Results can be narrowed by aspect-ratio range, size and quadrature tolerance. [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/vision/vndetectrectanglesrequest)
- Documented defaults: minimumAspectRatio 0.5, maximumAspectRatio 1.0, quadratureTolerance 30 degrees, minimumSize 0.2 of the smaller image dimension, minimumConfidence 0.0, maximumObservations 1 (0 means unlimited). [objc2-vision docs mirroring Apple headers](https://docs.rs/objc2-vision/0.3.2/objc2_vision/struct.VNDetectRectanglesRequest.html)
- VNTrackRectangleRequest exists for tracking a detected rectangle across frames. [objc2-vision docs](https://docs.rs/objc2-vision/0.3.2/objc2_vision/struct.VNTrackRectangleRequest.html)
- VNDetectDocumentSegmentationRequest (iOS 15) is ML-based, runs on the Neural Engine, GPU or CPU, and "finds one document" with a segmentation mask and corner points. VNDocumentCameraViewController (VisionKit) already wraps it. The third-party write-up contrasts it with VNDetectRectanglesRequest, which uses a CPU edge and intersection approach. [ITNEXT article](https://itnext.io/document-scanner-in-swiftui-709753af367b); [Apple forums thread on corners and mask](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/695858)
- The Swift-native DetectDocumentSegmentationRequest is listed from iOS 18 and returns a DetectedDocumentObservation. [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/vision/detectdocumentsegmentationrequest)
- WWDC24 moved Vision to Swift async/await and dropped the VN prefix. WWDC25 added a session "Read documents using the Vision framework". I found nothing in 2025 or 2026 sessions aimed at generic object or rectangle detection. [Kodeco Vision course](https://assets.carolus.raywenderlich.com/ios/paths/apple-ai-models/49307523-vision-framework/04-unveiling-new-vision-framework-features-in-ios-18-ios-26/01); [WWDC25 sessions](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/267/)
- Subject lifting: VNGenerateForegroundInstanceMaskRequest (iOS 17+) returns VNInstanceMaskObservation with per-instance masks. A forum reply says it does not run on the CPU and fails in the simulator. Apple forums suggest using it per frame for video. No latency figures found. [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/vision/vngenerateforegroundinstancemaskrequest.md); [Apple forums 764948](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764948); [Apple forums 763315](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/763315)
- ML Kit Document Scanner runs fully on-device, ships models through Google Play services, and needs no camera permission because it uses Play services' camera. Customization is limited to page count, gallery import and three editing modes. Google's page covers Android only and does not document access to detected corners. [Google ML Kit doc scanner](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/doc-scanner)
- Scanbot's comparison describes ML Kit's scanner as pre-built UI with fixed scanning logic, Android only, in beta. The source is a competing SDK vendor and undated. [Scanbot blog](https://scanbot.io/blog/ml-kit-vs-opencv-document-scanning-software/)
- ML Kit Object Detection and Tracking runs on iOS and Android, on a single image or live feed, tracks objects across frames, offers a five-category coarse classifier (home goods, fashion goods, food, plants, places), and accepts a custom TFLite classifier. It returns bounding boxes. Google gives no latency figures. [Google ML Kit object detection](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/object-detection)
- The Flutter `document_scan` package returns four corner points plus the processed image and leaves the UI to the developer. It uses Apple Vision on iOS and OpenCV on Android. Its comparison table says ML Kit wrappers download models and UI through Play services at first run. [pub.dev document_scan](https://pub.dev/packages/document_scan)

### Inferences
- A square sleeve has an aspect ratio of 1.0, at the top of VNDetectRectanglesRequest's default range. Raising minimumAspectRatio to about 0.85 and minimumSize to about 0.4 should reject books, posters and the room's door frames while keeping the sleeve. Test this; no source covers it.
- Glossy sleeves and shrinkwrap produce specular highlights that break the straight edges a CPU edge detector relies on. Busy cover art can also produce strong internal rectangles. Expect VNDetectRectanglesRequest to need a confidence gate and a fallback, not to work alone.
- The ML document segmenter is trained on paper documents. It may lock onto a white inner sleeve or a printed insert instead of the cover. Treat it as a candidate to benchmark, not a known fit.
- On Android, ML Kit's document scanner cannot sit in a live viewfinder flow, so it is out. OpenCV contour finding is the only first-party-free option that returns corners. ML Kit object detection gives a box at best, which is no better than a framing guide.

### Gaps
- No published accuracy or per-frame latency for VNDetectRectanglesRequest, the document segmenter, subject lifting or ML Kit object detection on any device, and nothing on glossy or shrinkwrapped square sleeves.
- No source confirms whether any 2026 Vision update (iOS 27) adds new object or rectangle APIs.

## Do modern encoders cope with uncropped frames well enough that a framing guide plus center crop is enough?

### Takeaway
The strongest current evidence (Meivar et al., October 2026) says global embeddings degrade sharply when the object fills a different fraction of the query than of the gallery image, and resolution explains only about a fifth of the drop. A framing guide attacks exactly that mismatch by making the sleeve fill the crop the way CAA art fills its own image. Cheap test-time multi-scale averaging adds about 3 points of R@1. Detection-based crop re-ranking adds about 21 points, but most of that comes from the gallery side, which CAA covers already solve because they are tight crops.

### Cited Findings
- Meivar, Kedem, Edenzon, Chechik and Avidan, "Beyond Resolution: Object-to-Image Ratio Mismatch in Instance Retrieval" (arXiv 2610.11489, 8 October 2026), tested 12 backbones including DINOv3-L, DINOv2-L/G, SigLIP-2-L and CLIP-L. Every backbone degrades as the camera-distance ratio between query and gallery grows. Models "lose 0.5 to 0.9 R@1 at the extremes", and SigLIP-2-L retains only 0.126 R@1 at a 3.3x distance gap. [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)
- In the same paper, resolution explains little of the drop. For DINOv3-L the cross-distance drop is 0.215 and the resolution-only residual is 0.040 (about 19%). Multi-scale architectures (Swin, FlexiViT, MViTv2) do not fix it. [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)
- The failure is asymmetric: "a query whose object is larger than the gallery's matches more reliably than the reverse", with mean MRR gaps from +0.020 (SigLIP-2-L) to +0.153 (FlexiViT-L). DINOv3-L scores 0.715 for tight queries against 0.668 for wide queries. [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)
- Mitigations on ILIAS 100M with DINOv3-L, baseline R@1 55.0 and mAP@1000 26.5: gray-padded 5-scale query averaging gives 58.2 and 28.3; masked 3-scale augmentation gives 59.2 and 29.2; generic augmentations at equal cost gain only 0.3 to 1.0 points; a LoRA fine-tune gives 60.2 and 29.7 in a single forward pass; OWLv2 crop re-ranking of the top 1000 gives 76.0 and 42.0. [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)
- The OWLv2 re-ranking used class-agnostic objectness with no text prompt (objectness above 0.02, top 50 boxes, about 31 boxes per gallery image on average). Each region was padded to square and encoded. Gallery-side cost was about 32x per image, cached once; query-side cost stayed at 1x. The authors note that cropping the query to the object box performs comparably to gray-pad averaging "but requires a bounding box". [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)
- ILIAS (CVPR 2025) finds clutter, occlusion and partial views to be a main difficulty for instance retrieval. Re-ranking with local descriptors beats global descriptors "by a significant margin". Foundation models brought a significant boost over time. [ILIAS paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Kordopatis-Zilos_ILIAS_Instance-Level_Image_retrieval_At_Scale_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)
- Pinterest's production visual search ran object detection before descriptor extraction (2015). Pinterest Lens, a camera product, applied detection across billions of catalog images so query objects could match objects inside catalog images (2017). A unified embedding later served Flashlight, Lens and Shop the Look (2019). These are older sources and concern multi-object scenes. [Visual Search at Pinterest](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.07647); [Visual Discovery at Pinterest](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.04680); [Unified Embedding at Pinterest](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.01707)
- DINOv3 beats DINOv2 by 10.8 points on the Met art retrieval benchmark and 7.6 on AmsterTime. [DINOv3 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10104)

### Inferences
- CAA images are full-bleed covers, the tightest possible gallery. A loose phone frame where the sleeve fills 40% of the image is the hard, wide-query direction in Meivar et al. A framing guide that makes the user fill a square overlay, followed by a crop to that overlay, turns the query into a near-tight match. Of the paper's numbers, this is the cheapest fix that applies to Record Scan.
- Most of the OWLv2 gain in that paper comes from cropping objects inside cluttered gallery images. Record Scan's gallery has no clutter, so the gain a query-side detector can add is closer to the "crop to box performs comparably to gray-pad averaging" result, about 3 to 4 points, than to 21 points. This is my reading, not a result the paper reports for this setting.
- A practical recipe follows: crop to the overlay, then embed the crop at 2 or 3 scales (the overlay crop, a 10% tighter crop, and a gray-padded version), average or max-pool the scores, and re-rank the top candidates. This costs a few extra encoder passes per scan, not a detector.
- Pinterest needed detection because its queries and catalog hold many objects. A single sleeve held up to the camera is the easy case those systems were working around.

### Gaps
- No paper or product write-up measures phone photos of album covers specifically. The Meivar numbers come from Objaverse renders and ILIAS, which hold mostly 3D objects and landmarks, not flat printed art with glare.
- I found no public detail on how Google Lens crops or re-ranks queries in 2026.
- Perspective skew is not covered by the O2I paper. How much a 20 to 30 degree tilt costs DINOv3 or SigLIP-2 on flat art is unmeasured in what I found.

## If a detector is still needed, what is the cheapest route?

### Takeaway
A one-class nano detector is cheap to run (YOLO26n detects in 3.7 ms on an iPhone 17 Pro Neural Engine) but expensive to label well by Ultralytics' own guidance. Zero-shot detectors are slow on phones (Grounding DINO takes about 1 second even on a Jetson GPU) and fit better as offline tools that pre-label photos for a nano model. A detector should be the last resort, after the framing guide and Apple's rectangle detector are measured.

### Cited Findings
- Ultralytics' CoreML page reports YOLO26n INT8 detection at 640 px on an iPhone 17 Pro (A19 Pro): 3.7 ms with CPU plus Neural Engine, 8.8 ms CPU only, mean of 15 runs, including pre- and post-processing. An older, pre-standard live-camera sweep on the same device measured 11.3 ms per frame. The page states no model size or mAP. YOLO26 is NMS-free; YOLO11 can embed CoreML NMS with `nms=True`. [Ultralytics CoreML docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/coreml)
- Conflict: a search snippet of the same Ultralytics page gave iOS 26.5.2, 3.8 ms ANE, 9.1 ms CPU and 11.3 ms live camera. The fetched page lists iOS 27.0.1 and calls the 11.3 ms figure historical. The page has been edited recently, so cite it as "about 4 ms single image, about 11 ms per live frame". [Ultralytics CoreML docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/coreml)
- Roboflow reports a case where CoreML export moved a YOLO11 model from 21 FPS in on-device PyTorch to 85 FPS on the Neural Engine. The device and model size are unstated. [Roboflow blog](https://blog.roboflow.com/best-ios-object-detection-models/)
- Ultralytics training guidance recommends at least 1,500 images per class, at least 10,000 labeled instances per class, and 0 to 10% background images to reduce false positives. This is guidance for best results, not a minimum for one easy class. [Ultralytics training tips](https://docs.ultralytics.com/yolov5/tutorials/tips_for_best_training_results/)
- YOLO-World uses "prompt-then-detect": text prompts are encoded ahead of time into an offline vocabulary, so the deployed detector needs no text encoder. The official repo lists TFLite INT8 export. [Ultralytics YOLO-World docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo-world/); [YOLO-World repo mirror](https://gitee.com/summry/YOLO-World)
- YOLOE extends YOLO with text, image or internal-vocabulary prompts for real-time open-vocabulary detection and segmentation, built on YOLOv10 and inspired by YOLO-World. [Ultralytics YOLOE docs](https://dagshub.com/Ultralytics/ultralytics/src/7bfd908900da705002a01c49102903ddf55c09a3/docs/en/models/yoloe.md)
- Grounding DINO measured 165.4 ms on an NVIDIA L4 at batch size 1 [Roboflow docs](https://docs.roboflow.com/models/supported-models/grounding-dino), and about 1,083 ms with TensorRT on a Jetson GPU and 13,580 ms on the Jetson CPU [arXiv 2607.11356](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11356). Grounding DINO 1.5 Edge targets low-power devices; I found no phone latency for it. [DeepLearning.AI summary](https://charonhub.deeplearning.ai/grounding-dino-1-5-an-edge-device-model-built-for-faster-smarter-object-detection/)
- OWLv2 works as a class-agnostic objectness proposer with no text prompt (see the Meivar et al. findings above). [arXiv 2610.11489](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11489v1)

### Inferences
- Cheapest detector route if one proves necessary: shoot a few hundred phone photos of real sleeves, auto-label them with OWLv2 or Grounding DINO on a desktop GPU (prompt "album cover" or plain objectness), hand-correct the boxes, then fine-tune YOLO26n with one class. One rigid, high-contrast, square class is far easier than COCO, so a few hundred to about a thousand images is a plausible starting point, below Ultralytics' general 1,500. This is an estimate, not a sourced figure.
- An oriented-box (OBB) or 4-keypoint pose head would return corners for perspective correction, which a plain box cannot. YOLO26n OBB and pose run at about 4 ms on the iPhone 17 Pro per the Ultralytics table, so corners cost little extra at runtime. They cost more at labeling time.
- YOLO-World or YOLOE with a baked-in "album cover" vocabulary is the only zero-shot option likely to run in real time on a phone. Its exported mobile latency is unverified, and it still returns a box, not corners.
- Recommended order: (1) framing guide and overlay crop with multi-scale query embedding, measured on a 200 to 500 photo eval set; (2) add VNDetectRectanglesRequest with tight aspect and size limits on iOS, plus an OpenCV contour pass on Android, applied only when confident and with the overlay crop as fallback; (3) train a nano detector or keypoint model only if eval shows recall lost to framing or skew that steps 1 and 2 do not recover. The lost Supabase box data is not needed for step 1 or step 2.

### Gaps
- No COCO mAP for YOLO26n on the Ultralytics CoreML page, and no head-to-head YOLO11n vs YOLO26n vs RT-DETR nano numbers on the same phone.
- No phone latency found for OWLv2, Grounding DINO 1.5 Edge, YOLO-World or YOLOE, and no confirmation that YOLOE exports to CoreML with prompts baked in.
- No published labeled-image count for fine-tuning a single-class nano detector to a stated mAP; the figure above is an estimate.
- No Android first-party latency numbers (ML Kit, LiteRT) for nano detectors on a named Pixel or Snapdragon device.
