Teaching a phone to recognize a record

Point your phone at a record sleeve. Within about a second it should name the album and play thirty seconds of it. This page explains where the project stands, what the first attempt got wrong, and the twelve steps that lead to a phone app. It assumes no machine-learning background.

The raw material is in good shape. Over three days in August 2025 the pipeline downloaded a front cover for almost every MusicBrainz release that has one, and kept the title and artist for each. That is the catalog the app will match against.

The prototype works end to end but it asks a large language model to look at each photo, which costs money per scan and takes a few seconds. It is a stand-in. The real job is to build our own identifier that runs on the phone, which is what the rest of this page is about.

This week the code was consolidated into one repository, the data was moved out of git, and the pipeline was given a configuration layer and tests. The research report this page summarizes was written on 24 October 2026.

The core idea: pictures as points

A recognition model does not "look things up". It turns every image into a list of a few hundred numbers. Think of that list as coordinates: each image becomes a point in a very high-dimensional space. The model is trained so that images that look alike land close together and images that look different land far apart.

That turns identification into geometry. We run all 3 million covers through the model once and store their points. When you take a photo, it becomes a point too, and the answer is simply the stored point nearest to it.

What went wrong last time

Photos are messy. A tilted sleeve under a lamp does not look like the clean image in the catalog, so its point lands some distance away. The November 2025 design tried to fix that on the storage side: for every cover it stored not one point but 13.6 on average, one for each fake variation (rotated, skewed, brightened, blurred). The hope was that a real photo would land near at least one of them.

The arithmetic kills it. 3.13 million covers times 13.6 variations is 42.5 million points. Held in memory in the structure that makes nearest-neighbor search fast, that is roughly 100 GB. The Mac doing the work has 64 GB. A phone has a small fraction of that.

The new idea: teach the model, not the index

Store exactly one point per cover, the clean one. Put all the tolerance for tilt, glare and blur into the model instead, so that a messy photo of a sleeve lands on the clean cover's point by itself. The report calls this moving the work "from the gallery side to the query side", and it is the single biggest change in the plan.

How do you teach a model to ignore glare? By showing it millions of examples where the only difference between two images is glare. We do not own millions of real phone photos of records, but we own 3 million clean covers, and a few lines of code can turn any clean cover into a plausible fake phone photo: tilt it, add a bright streak, darken one corner, blur it slightly, drop it onto a shelf background.

Why that generalizes to real photos

The model never stores any of the fake photos. It has no slot to keep them in. The only thing that changes during training is one shared set of weights, and the only way the score keeps improving across millions of different covers and different random edits is for those weights to learn general operations: undo a tilt, ignore a bright streak, discount the background. Tilt is tilt whatever artwork is under it, so a tolerance learned on one cover applies to every cover, including ones the model never saw. Face recognition works the same way: trained on millions of people under varying light, it recognizes a new face in new light because "lighting" became something the weights cancel out.

Three levels of effort, used only as needed

  • Frozen. Use a published model exactly as it comes. Costs nothing, and if it is already good enough on real photos the plan skips training entirely.
  • Head. Keep the model frozen and train a small adapter on top of its output. Runs on the Mac in hours. Also tells us whether a full fine-tune has room to help.
  • Fine-tune, then distill. Retrain the big model on the fake-photo pairs (a few hours of rented GPU, about $10 to $60), then teach a much smaller "student" model to copy its answers so it can run on a phone in about 10 milliseconds ($2 to $15).

Two safety rules carry through all three. Covers that are pixel-identical across several releases are grouped first, so the training never tries to push identical art apart. And your real photos never enter training; they are the only honest test of whether any of this worked.

Which model to start with

Several published models can serve as the starting point. The research compared them on a standard test of recognizing the same object across different photos, where higher is better. Nothing newer than these appeared between November 2025 and now; the old pipeline simply never tried them.

Why DINOv3. It was trained to tell instances apart without text labels, which is exactly the skill matching a specific artwork needs, and Meta published small distilled versions (21M and 29M parameters) that are phone-sized. Its license allows shipping the weights in an app as long as the license text is bundled.

Why keep one vision-language model in the race. Models like SigLIP 2 learned from captions, so they can read text in images. DINOv3 cannot read. Some covers are mostly typography, and many reissues share artwork and differ only in a printed line, so a reading model might win on that slice. Rather than argue about how many covers are "text-heavy", the evaluation reports that slice separately and the data decides.

How it runs on your phone

Measured numbers from 2025 and 2026 phones settle one worry: turning a photo into a point is fast. A model of the size we are considering runs in 10 to 21 milliseconds on recent Snapdragon chips, and Apple reports similar figures for its own small encoders. Searching 3 million stored points is the part a phone struggles with, so the plan starts with the search on a server and moves it onto the phone only once a compressed index proves it loses almost nothing.

Finding the sleeve in the frame does not need its own trained detector, at least to start. The app draws a square guide, you line the sleeve up with it, and the crop is the query. An October 2026 study found that the main cause of lost matches is the object filling a different share of the frame in the photo than in the catalog image, and a guide fixes exactly that. The lost annotation data from last year is not needed for this.

What each part of the phone does

A modern iPhone or Android flagship has four kinds of compute, and the plan leans on each for a different job. The export spike in Step 4 is where the assignments get measured instead of assumed.

Neural Engine (NPU)

Runs the small model that turns a frame into a point. This is where the 10 to 20 ms figures come from. Core ML on iOS and the Qualcomm or MediaTek backends on Android place the model here; Step 4 checks that every layer actually lands on it, because one unsupported layer falls back to the GPU or CPU and can triple the time.

CPU

Runs the nearest-neighbor search if the index moves onto the phone (libraries like USearch use the CPU's vector instructions), the camera cropping, and the few hundred microseconds of glue between stages. Also the fallback for any model layer the NPU refuses.

GPU

Resizing and color conversion of camera frames, and a second fallback for model layers. Not the preferred home for the model: on phones the NPU is faster per watt for this size of network, and the GPU is shared with the camera preview.

RAM

Holds the model (about 40 to 60 MB in half precision for a 20 to 30M parameter student) and, in version 2, the compressed index (0.1 to 0.2 GB, memory-mapped so the OS can page it). Well inside what iOS lets a foreground app keep; Step 4 records peak memory per device.

Two things the hardware list does not settle, and the plan should measure alongside Step 4: battery and heat under continuous scanning at 5 to 10 frames per second (the fix is to scan at a lower rate until something sleeve-like is in the guide), and the first-launch cost, since compiling a model for the Neural Engine can take minutes on the newest iPhones and must be cached after the first run.

The twelve steps

Each step measures something, and the measurement decides what happens next. Nobody commits to the expensive steps until the cheap ones say they are worth it. Steps 0 to 4 cost nothing but time. Steps 5, 8 and 9 rent a GPU for under $100 in total.

Phase A · free, on the Mac and your phone
  1. 0

    Build the gold set

    Photograph 60 to 100 of your own records, 3 to 5 shots each, under deliberately varied conditions, plus 100 to 200 frames with no record in them. Teach the scorer to count a near-identical cover as a hit and to report recall per slice (text-only, dark, glare, hand in frame).

    Decides: nothing yet. Every later number depends on it. With 40 photos the error bars are about 12 points wide; with 300 they are about 4.5.

    your phonemac
  2. 1

    One pass over the drive

    Read all 3.13M covers once. Write small 256-pixel copies into shards (50 to 80 GB) and compute a fingerprint for each so identical artwork across releases can be grouped.

    Decides: the real gallery size, which sets every index size. Likely 2 to 2.5 million after grouping, but nobody has measured it.

    macneeds the drive
  3. 2

    Try the published models as they are

    Embed the gold covers plus 50,000 random distractors with each candidate model and query with your photos. One point per image, no tricks.

    Decides: two "teacher" candidates and two phone-sized "student" candidates. Exit ramp: if a small frozen model already clears 90% top-1 and 97% top-5, skip training (Steps 7 to 9) and ship it.

    macneeds the drive
  4. 3

    Check whether the square guide is enough

    Compare the plain guide crop, a multi-scale version, Apple's built-in rectangle detector and an OpenCV outline pass. Label every miss by its cause.

    Decides: if framing and skew explain under a quarter of the remaining misses, the custom detector track is closed for good.

    macyour phone
  5. 4

    Export the small models to phones before training them

    Convert each student candidate to Core ML and to the Android runtimes. Measure speed, cold start, memory, and that the phone produces the same point as the Mac.

    Decides: which students are shippable. Anything over 30 ms on the oldest target phone, or that fails to convert, drops out before any GPU money is spent on it.

    macyour phone
Phase B · first working system
  1. 5

    Embed the whole gallery and go live, hybrid

    Rent a GPU for about an hour per candidate, embed every cover, and rerun the gold test at full scale. Serve the index from the Mac. In this first version the phone uploads a cropped JPEG and the server does the embedding, so the app works before any student model exists.

    Decides: the teacher. A big accuracy drop from 50,000 distractors to the full gallery means near-duplicates are hurting and Step 8 must mine them.

    rented gpumacneeds the drive
  2. 6

    Build the fake-photo simulator and calibrate it

    Write the tilt-glare-shadow-blur-background pipeline. Then check it against your real photos: the model should fail on fake photos about as often, and on the same slices, as on real ones.

    Decides: the simulator's settings are frozen once fake and real recall are within 5 points. If fake photos are much easier, widen glare, tilt and clutter until they are not.

    mac
  3. 7

    Train a small adapter on the frozen teacher

    Cache the teacher's output for 100,000 to 200,000 covers and their fake photos, and train a small head that also shrinks each point to 256 numbers.

    Decides: the point size, and whether Step 8 is worth it. A gain of 5 points or more means a full fine-tune has headroom; under 2 means skip it.

    mac
Phase C · training, only as needed
  1. 8

    Fine-tune the teacher

    Only if Step 7 showed headroom. Retrain the whole teacher on clean-cover-to-fake-photo pairs, with identical-artwork groups kept out of the "push apart" set. About $10 to $60 of GPU time.

    Decides: adopt it only if it beats Step 7 by 3 points and loses no slice. Otherwise keep the frozen teacher plus adapter.

    rented gpu
  2. 9

    Distill the phone student

    Teach each surviving small model to map fake photos onto the teacher's clean-cover points. 3 to 10 hours on one consumer GPU, $2 to $15. The phone then sends a 512-byte point instead of a photo.

    Decides: which student ships. It must stay within 2 points of the teacher and under 30 ms on the oldest target phone.

    rented gpuyour phone
Phase D · finishing
  1. 10

    Try moving the index onto the phone

    Build compressed indexes (product quantization at 64 and 32 bytes per point, and a binary variant) and query them with the student on the gold set.

    Decides: if the 64-byte index stays within 2 points of exact search, ship it as a 0.1 to 0.2 GB download with the server as fallback. Otherwise stay hybrid.

    macneeds the drive
  2. 11

    Calibrate the lock and finish the app

    Pick the confidence threshold that gives 99% precision when the app commits to an answer, require three agreeing frames, and send low-confidence text-heavy cases through on-device text recognition against artist and title.

    Decides: the ship bar. 99% lock precision, 85% of gold records locked within 3 seconds, and a blank wall never locks.

    your phone

What nobody has measured yet

  • How DINOv3 performs on our covers, or at 224 pixels, or in its ConvNeXt form. Public benchmarks are not album covers. Step 2 answers this.
  • How fast DINOv3 or SigLIP 2 actually run on an iPhone or a Pixel. Only Apple's and Qualcomm's own small models have published phone timings. Step 4 answers this.
  • How much accuracy product quantization costs on near-duplicate artwork. Step 10 answers this.
  • Whether Apple's MobileCLIP2 license allows use in a public app. Someone has to read it.
  • Every threshold in the plan (2 points, 5 points, 30 ms) is the report author's judgment, chosen to sit outside the gold set's noise, not a number from a paper.

Glossary

Embedding, or point
The list of a few hundred numbers a model produces for an image. Treated as coordinates, so "similar" means "nearby".
Gallery, or index
All the stored cover points, arranged in a structure that makes finding the nearest one fast without comparing against all 3 million.
Query
Your photo, after it has been turned into a point. The app looks for the gallery point nearest to it.
Recall at 1, recall at 5
The share of test photos where the correct album is the top answer, or is among the top five answers.
Distractors
Random extra covers added to a test gallery so the model has to pick the right answer out of a crowd, not just out of the handful we photographed.
Frozen model
A published model used exactly as downloaded, with no training of our own.
Fine-tuning
Continuing to train a published model on our own examples so it gets better at our specific task.
Teacher and student
A big accurate model (teacher) and a small fast one trained to copy its answers (student). The student is what runs on the phone.
Distillation
The training process that makes the student copy the teacher.
Simulator
Our code that turns a clean cover into a fake phone photo: tilt, glare, shadow, blur, a background. Produces training pairs on the fly; nothing is saved.
Product quantization
A way to compress each stored point from about a kilobyte to 32 or 64 bytes while keeping nearest-neighbor search mostly accurate. It is what could let the whole gallery fit on a phone.
Lock
The moment the app stops scanning and commits to an answer, because the same album has won several frames in a row with a high enough score.
